## Section: Case study

**Heading**
> Telling guests the right thing, first time, every morning.

**Standfirst (one line, above the block)**
> A Birmingham short-stay host. Two city-centre apartments. Dozens of guest
> messages a week. One small automation that drafts every reply before
> breakfast.

**The problem**
> A client runs two short-let apartments in Birmingham — one a city centre stay
> with secure parking. Guests message constantly: check-in times, parking,
> what's nearby, the small practical things that decide whether a stay gets a
> five-star review.
>
> Every message had to be read, understood and answered by hand. Miss one, or
> answer it late and vaguely, and it shows up in the review score — which is the
> whole business.

**What we did**
> We built a small system that reads the guest messages each morning from the
> booking platform, matches each one against the property's own house rules and
> local knowledge, and writes a clear, plain-text reply for each. Every morning
> at 07:30 the host gets a short digest: here are today's messages, here are the
> suggested replies.
>
> A person still reads and approves every single one before it goes anywhere.
> The automation does the reading and the drafting; the judgement stays human.

**The result**
> - Replies are drafted daily at 07:30, for both apartments, in one digest.
> - First live run: **4 out of 4** guest messages answered correctly, first try.
> - No guest is left waiting on a message nobody saw.
> - The host's morning routine went from "work through the inbox" to
>   "read the digest, approve, done."

**How it was built** *(the credibility line — reuse the real constraints)*
> Read-only access to the booking platform. No scraping, no shortcuts. Plain
> text only, because the platform forbids links and images in some guest
> messages — so the drafts respect the rules by design. Guest data is used to
> answer that guest and nothing else.

**CTA**
> Got a job like this? → [describe your problem] / [talk to a human]

## Why this works as a case study

- **It's the same audience.** A small business drowning in repetitive messages
  instantly recognises the problem. That recognition sells harder than any
  feature list.
- **It proves the human-in-the-loop promise.** The paid-call offer
  (`sorted-uplift-and-booking-plan.md`) says a person stays in charge. This is
  the evidence — the automation drafts, a human approves.
- **It matches the free-tool positioning.** The product did the work; the
  human time was Banks's own. Nothing here implies a paywall.
- **It's honest about scope.** Two apartments, one task, done well — which reads
  as more credible than "we transform businesses."
- **Nothing to ask permission for.** No name, no quote, no client sign-off —
  publishable now.

## Trade-off of staying anonymous

- **Cost:** anonymous case studies are less verifiable, so they read a little
  more like a claim than a reference. Fine at one case study; a problem if all
  of them are anonymous.
- **Offset:** the specifics do the work — two apartments, Birmingham, 07:30,
  4 out of 4. Concrete detail is what makes an unnamed story feel real.
- **If you ever want the named version**, the earlier draft (consent + one real
  quote) is a stronger asset than this one. Worth revisiting when you have a
  client relationship you can spend a favour on. Not now.
