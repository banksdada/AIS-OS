# Building a Clear AI Strategy for Your School

Updated: 2026-11-12T09:19:48

Most schools we work with don’t have an AI strategy at all – they have pockets of enthusiasm. Maybe a head of department using ChatGPT to draft correspondence. Maybe a teacher quietly trialling an AI marking tool. Meanwhile, governors and parents are asking questions and nobody has a consistent answer. This isn’t a criticism of busy teachers. It’s the natural result of fast-moving tools landing on already-stretched staff. The schools that get ahead now will be the ones that help teachers reclaim time, streamline admin and spend more of their energy on pupils. Here’s a five-step approach that actually works in a school setting. 1. Audit what’s already happening Before you write a single policy document, find out what’s already in use. Run a quick anonymous staff survey. Ask: which AI tools are you using, for what, and how often? You’ll almost certainly discover more activity than expected. That’s useful. It tells you where quick wins might live and where guidance is most urgently needed. 2. Pick three use cases, not thirty AI works best when it’s solving a named, recurring problem. Generic “we should use AI across the school” plans stall because they’re too broad. Strong first use cases in schools tend to cluster around: Admin reduction – drafting parent letters, meeting agendas, trip risk assessments, policy summaries. Teaching support – lesson plan starters, differentiating worksheets, generating quiz questions. Comms and reporting – newsletter drafts, social media ideas, summaries for governor reports. Pick two or three and commit to them properly before adding more. 3. Sort data protection and safeguarding from day one This is the step most schools skip, and the one that derails everything later. Anything you do with AI in a school setting touches GDPR, child data and safeguarding duties. Document three things early: What data can and can’t be entered into external AI tools. Which tools have appropriate contracts (DPA, age-appropriate terms) for school use. How staff should handle any output that goes near a child or family. Without this nailed down, you can’t safely scale beyond your early adopters – and your early adopters will quietly keep going without oversight. 4. Pilot with a small, willing group Forget the all-staff rollout. Pick a small group of two to four staff who actually want to be involved, agree on the use cases from step two, give them protected time to test, and capture what they learn. A good pilot answers simple questions: did it save time? Was the output usable? What surprised us? What blocked us? After roughly a half term you’ll have honest, school-shaped evidence rather than vendor promises. 5. Write the roadmap – then share it A strategy without a roadmap is just an opinion. Once your pilot has produced real findings, turn them into a one-page roadmap covering the next 12 months: what gets rolled out, what stays off-limits, who owns what, how staff get trained, and how success is measured. Then share it. With staff, with governors, ideally with families. An AI strategy you only share internally still gets rumour, suspicion, and a flurry of well-meaning WhatsApp messages.
