# Hotel Website Redesign: How to Stop Losing Direct Bookings to OTAs

Updated: 2026-11-02T14:08:41

If you run a hotel, guest house, B&B, or serviced apartment block, your website is your most profitable booking channel. Every booking that comes through your own site (rather than Booking.com, Expedia, or Airbnb) keeps you from paying 15–25% in commission. Yet most independent and small-group hotels still bleed bookings to OTAs because their websites are slow, ugly on phones, hard to navigate, or fail to inspire trust. A good hotel website redesign fixes that. Here is what to focus on in 2026. 1. Mobile-first, not just mobile-friendly The majority of travel research and bookings now happen on a phone. That means your site must be designed mobile-first, not just “responsive.” Tap targets should be generous, forms short, and room photos fast-loading. Test it the way guests do: stand in your reception, open your site on your own phone over hotel Wi-Fi, and try to book a room in under three minutes. If you can’t, your guests can’t either. 2. Make the booking engine impossible to miss The “Book Now” button should be visible above the fold on every page, not buried in the menu. It should open your booking engine directly rather than bouncing through a “Check Availability” form that asks for dates before showing rates. A two-step flow beats a five-step one. The more friction you remove, the more direct bookings you keep. 3. Speed matters more than you think Slow sites lose bookings. Aim for a Largest Contentful Paint under 2.5 seconds on mobile. Compress hero images to WebP or AVIF. Lazy-load everything below the fold. If you are unsure where you stand, run your site through Google’s PageSpeed Insights before and after the redesign and compare. Most hotel sites we audit in London see double-digit performance improvements simply by switching hosts, image formats, and removing a few bloated plugins. 4. Photography that actually sells the room Stock photos of lobbies tell guests nothing. Commission professional photography of every room type, the breakfast spread, the spa, the view from Room 407. Show real bathrooms. Show the actual bed. Add a short, calm walkthrough video on the homepage. A 30-second clip of someone walking into a room and dropping their bag does more selling than five paragraphs of copy. 5. Local SEO and your Google Business Profile When travellers search “hotel near [attraction]” or “boutique hotel [city],” you want to appear. That means: A complete, current Google Business Profile with fresh photos Location pages on your site for each property or nearby landmark Hotel schema markup so Google can show your star rating, price range, and availability directly in search If you run more than one property, give each one its own page. Don’t squeeze four hotels under a single shared URL. 6. Reviews, ratings, and trust signals Travellers book with confidence. Display your Tripadvisor, Booking.com, and Google scores prominently, but link them to real, verifiable review pages. Embed a few recent guest quotes on the homepage. Add trust signals: your cancellation policy in plain English, secure-payment badges, accessibility information, and clear pricing with taxes and fees included. Hidden fees are the single fastest way to lose a direct booking to an OTA. 7. Pages for every guest type Business travellers, families, couples, and wedding parties all need different information. Give each a clear landing page rather than making them hunt through a generic “facilities” menu. 8. Events, weddings, and restaurant enquiries If you host weddings, conferences, or run a restaurant, those revenue streams deserve their own sections with enquiry forms, not just a phone number. Restaurant pages should include menus, opening hours, and a direct reservation link. 9. Multi-language if you serve international guests If you take overseas bookings, even a basic three-language site (English plus your top two source markets) outperforms English-only by a wide margin. Use professional translation, not machine output, for booking flows and key pages. 10. Accessibility, not as an afterthought Accessible sites rank better, convert better, and serve more guests. Use sufficient colour contrast, descriptive alt text for room photos, captioned video, and keyboard-navigable booking flows. Aim for at least WCAG 2.1 AA. 11. Analytics and conversion tracking A redesigned site without analytics is guesswork. Set up Google Analytics 4, track the booking funnel step by step, and watch where guests drop off. Connect your booking engine so you can attribute revenue back to traffic sources, then double down on what works. 12. A CMS your team can actually use Front-desk managers and marketing teams should not need a developer to update room rates, post a new package, or upload wedding photos. WordPress, Webflow, or a well-configured headless setup all work, as long as the editorial experience is genuinely simple.
