# How Often Should You Redesign Your CIC Website? A Practical Guide for 2026

CIC websites have a habit of going quiet. They get launched with a small grant, a willing volunteer, or a well-meaning freelancer, and then sit untouched for years while the organisation keeps growing underneath them. If you’re running a Community Interest Company, your website is doing more heavy lifting than most. It’s where funders check you’re legitimate, where beneficiaries find your services, where local authorities verify your details, and where the occasional press contact lands. When that site looks dated, loads slowly, or doesn’t work well on a phone, every one of those audiences quietly loses a bit of trust. So how often should you redesign your CIC website? The honest answer is: it depends, but less often than you’d guess for the visual stuff, and more often than you’d guess for the strategy underneath. The Suggested Lifespan of a CIC Website Most small-organisation websites genuinely last somewhere between three and five years before a proper redesign pays for itself. Anything older than that and you’re usually fighting the platform, not using it. But “redesign” doesn’t have to mean a full rebuild every time. A useful way to think about it: Every 12 months: a content and SEO review. New pages, updated service descriptions, fresh case studies, fixed broken links. This is the single biggest ROI activity most CICs skip. Every 2-3 years: a design refresh. New colours, updated imagery, modernised typography. Usually light work that can happen without touching the underlying build. Every 4-5 years: a full redesign or rebuild on a modern platform. New structure, new features, mobile-first thinking baked in from day one. Owners who treat this as a rolling cycle rather than a one-off project end up spending less overall, because each phase is smaller and cheaper than the “everything at once” rebuild. Signs Your CIC Website Is Overdue Not sure where you sit on the timeline? These are the patterns we see again and again at WebAwesome: It’s not properly mobile-responsive, or it works on phones but feels fiddly and slow. The homepage still leads with a stock photo from when the company was founded. Form submissions go missing, or staff manually re-type enquiries from the website into another system. You can’t easily update it yourself, so small changes sit in a queue for weeks. Funders or partners have mentioned the site looks “a bit tired” more than once. You don’t appear in Google for your own service area, even when people search your organisation name. If two or more of those ring true, you’re probably due a real conversation about your website, not just another content tweak. What Actually Changes in a Modern CIC Website A good redesign isn’t really about looking prettier. It’s about doing a few specific jobs better: Trust signals front and centre. Your CIC number, registrations, safeguarding info, real photos of real staff and beneficiaries. Clear service pathways. Visitors should reach “How we can help” in a single click, not three. Working forms that integrate with your CRM or email. No more lost referrals. Accessibility as a baseline. WCAG-aware layout, readable fonts, good colour contrast, captions on video. Performance. Pages that load quickly on a cheap Android over a slow 4G connection, because that’s reality for a lot of your audience. If you want a deeper look at what separates a site that just looks nice from one that actually delivers, our piece on functional websites that convert, not just beautiful ones is worth a read. A Real Example: Redesigning risegencic.org We’re currently working through a full redesign of www.risegencic.org , and it’s a textbook case of the points above. The previous site had grown organically over several years, content was solid, but the navigation didn’t reflect how the organisation actually operates today, the mobile experience was clunky, and several key service pages weren’t surfacing in search. The rebuild focuses less on a visual overhaul and more on the plumbing: clearer service pathways, faster load times, accessible by default, and content structured so the in-house team can keep it fresh without calling in a developer. It’s the kind of work that, done well, should comfortably carry the site through to its next planned refresh in two to three years. A Simple Annual Checklist Set aside half a day once a year and walk through this: Look at your homepage on your phone over a slow connection. Honest verdict? Submit your main enquiry form. Did it arrive where it should? Search Google for your two most important service phrases. Are you visible? Check your top three pages against current WCAG accessibility basics. Ask two people outside the organisation to find a specific piece of information in under a minute. If you can’t get through that list without making notes, it’s probably time for a redesign conversation.
