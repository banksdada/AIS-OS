# Why AI Leadership Buy-In Is the Hardest Part of Any AI Project (And How to Get It)

Source: https://www.webawesome.co.uk/why-ai-leadership-buy-in-is-the-hardest-part-of-any-ai-project-and-how-to-get-it/
Updated: 2026-11-12T09:53:24

Most AI projects don’t fail because the technology doesn’t work. They fail because the wrong conversation happened six months earlier in a meeting room, with someone who could fund the work but didn’t fully own it. If you’ve ever watched an AI initiative stall halfway through, get quietly shelved, or “pilot forever” without ever reaching production, the root cause is almost always the same: shallow leadership buy-in. Here’s why it matters, what real buy-in looks like, and how to actually get it. What buy-in actually means (and why most of it isn’t) There’s a big difference between approval and buy-in . Approval is: “Sure, go ahead, sounds interesting.” Buy-in is: “I have personally committed to make this succeed, and I have thought through what that will cost my team, my budget, and my existing priorities.” Real buy-in means your sponsor: Has carved out real budget, not “see what you can do with what’s left” Will defend the project when something else competes for attention Has cleared internal political space for it Will use the thing themselves, not just sign it off If you don’t have those four, you don’t have buy-in. You have permission to struggle. Why AI is uniquely hard to get buy-in for AI projects sit at an awkward intersection. They are expensive enough that they need senior sign-off, but vague enough that signature-holders can’t easily evaluate them. Compare that to something more familiar, like a website redesign. The leadership team knows roughly what they’re paying for: a new site, faster, prettier, more conversions. With AI, leaders often hear phrases like “explore potential use cases” or “build capability for future opportunities.” That is not a brief. That is a punt. Add in the genuine fear of being left behind by competitors using AI, plus the fear of getting burned by a half-working tool, and you get the worst combination: urgency without clarity. That is where most AI projects lose their way. The four people you actually need on side You almost never need the whole board. You need four specific types of sponsor: The funder. Whoever actually controls the budget and won’t get their hand forced later. The operator. The senior person whose team will live with the result day-to-day. If they don’t want it, it won’t get used. The sceptic. The person most likely to ask “why are we doing this?” in a meeting six weeks from now. Win them over early, or you’ll relitigate the decision forever. The technical translator. Someone who can explain what AI can and can’t do in plain language, so the others aren’t making decisions on vibes. If your AI conversation only involves the funder, you’ve already lost. How to earn buy-in when AI is new to your organisation Start with a problem the leadership team already cares about. Not “we should use AI.” That is a tech-first pitch and it almost always dies. A business-first pitch sounds like: “We’re losing hours every week per support agent copying data between two systems. We’ve found a way to automate that. Here is what it would cost, and here is what we would save.” Three things that reliably help: Pick a small, ugly, expensive problem. Not the moonshot. The thing that is currently wasting money in a measurable way. Bring a partner who has done it before. Internal champions often get steamrollered. An outside perspective carries weight, de-risks the decision, and stops the project from being a referendum on the sponsor’s judgement. WebAwesome can help you get started by framing the work in business terms before a line of code is written. Mention what will happen if you do nothing. Competitors are moving. Costs are quietly growing. Customer expectations are shifting. The cost of inaction is usually higher than the cost of the project. Make that explicit. What good buy-in looks like in practice Good buy-in is boring. It is a written brief with a clear outcome, a real budget line, a named executive sponsor with their reputation attached, and a date when you’ll review whether it worked. If your AI project is missing any of those, fix that before you touch any technology.
